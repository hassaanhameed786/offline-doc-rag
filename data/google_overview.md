# Google: A Company Overview

## Origins

Google began as a research project at Stanford University in the mid-1990s. Two PhD students, Larry Page and Sergey Brin, wanted to find a better way to rank pages on the early web. Their first search engine was nicknamed BackRub, because it analyzed the links pointing back to a page. The key idea was an algorithm called PageRank, which treated a link from one page to another as a vote of confidence. Pages that received links from many important pages were ranked higher than pages that were rarely referenced.

In 1998 the pair incorporated the company and renamed it Google, a play on the word "googol", which is the number one followed by one hundred zeros. The name reflected their goal of organizing a huge amount of information. The first office was a rented garage in Menlo Park, California, and the company soon moved to Mountain View, where its headquarters, known as the Googleplex, is still located.

## Early growth and the business model

Google grew quickly because its search results felt more relevant than those of competitors, and its homepage was famously plain and fast to load. The company needed a way to pay for its servers and staff. It found one in advertising. In 2000 it launched AdWords, a system that let businesses bid for text ads shown next to search results. The ads were tied to the words people searched for, which made them more useful than banner ads, and advertisers paid only when someone clicked. Later, AdSense extended this model to third-party websites, letting site owners earn money by displaying relevant ads.

Google held its initial public offering in August 2004. It used an unusual auction-style process instead of the traditional method of letting investment banks allocate shares. The IPO made the company a public one and made many early employees wealthy.

## Expanding beyond search

After the IPO, Google launched a series of products that went far beyond web search.

Gmail arrived in April 2004 with a gigabyte of free storage, which was enormous at the time, since competing email services offered only a few megabytes. Google Maps launched in 2005 and brought smooth, draggable maps to the browser. Google Earth, based on satellite imagery, followed soon after. In 2006 Google acquired YouTube, the video-sharing site, for about 1.65 billion dollars in stock. YouTube became the largest online video platform in the world and a major source of advertising revenue.

Google Docs, Sheets and Slides brought word processing, spreadsheets and presentations into the web browser and allowed several people to edit the same file at once. These tools were later bundled with Gmail, Calendar and Drive into a suite for businesses and schools, now known as Google Workspace.

## Android and Chrome

In 2005 Google bought a small startup called Android Inc., which was building an operating system for mobile devices. Google released Android as an open-source platform in 2008, and phone makers around the world adopted it. Android became the most widely used mobile operating system, running on billions of devices. Google earns money from it indirectly through search, advertising and its app store, Google Play.

The Chrome web browser launched in 2008. It stood out for its speed and for running each tab as a separate process, so that a crash in one page would not bring down the entire browser. Chrome grew to become the most popular browser on desktop and mobile. Its open-source foundation, Chromium, also powers several other browsers.

## Alphabet and the "other bets"

In 2015 Google reorganized itself under a new parent company named Alphabet. Google remained the largest part of Alphabet and contained the core internet businesses: search, advertising, Android, Chrome, YouTube, Maps and the cloud business. Other ventures that were less related to the main business became separate companies under the Alphabet umbrella. These experimental projects were called "other bets". Examples include Waymo, which develops self-driving vehicles, and Verily, which focuses on life sciences and health research. The restructuring was meant to give each venture more independence and make the finances of the core business clearer to investors.

## Cloud computing

Google runs some of the largest data centers in the world to power its own products, and it began selling access to that infrastructure to other organizations. Google Cloud offers computing, storage, databases, analytics and machine-learning tools. Customers include startups, large enterprises and government agencies. Cloud computing has become one of the company's major growth areas, competing with other large providers. One of its notable technologies is Kubernetes, an open-source system for managing containerized applications, which Google originally developed from its internal cluster management experience and then released to the public in 2014.

## Artificial intelligence research

Artificial intelligence has been central to Google for many years. In 2011 a group of engineers started Google Brain, a research team focused on deep neural networks. In 2014 Google acquired DeepMind, a London-based AI lab. DeepMind became well known in 2016 when its program AlphaGo defeated Lee Sedol, one of the world's top players of the board game Go, a result that many experts had expected to be years away. Later, DeepMind's AlphaFold system made major progress on predicting the three-dimensional structure of proteins from their amino acid sequences, which is an important problem in biology and drug discovery.

In 2015 Google released TensorFlow, an open-source library for building and training machine-learning models, which quickly became one of the most widely used tools in the field. In 2017 Google researchers published a paper titled "Attention Is All You Need", which introduced the Transformer architecture. The Transformer replaced older recurrent designs with a mechanism called self-attention, allowing models to process all the words in a sentence in parallel and to capture long-range relationships between words. This architecture became the foundation of modern large language models, including those built by many other companies. In 2018 Google released BERT, a Transformer-based model that improved how search understands the meaning and context of queries.

Google also designs its own chips for machine learning. The Tensor Processing Unit, or TPU, is a custom processor built to speed up neural network training and inference in its data centers.

## Hardware

Google entered consumer hardware with the Nexus phones and later introduced the Pixel line in 2016. Pixel phones are known for their cameras, which rely heavily on computational photography, using software to combine multiple images and improve quality. Google also sells smart-home products under the Nest brand, such as thermostats, speakers and security cameras, and it makes earbuds and smartwatches.

## Culture and principles

Google became known for its workplace culture, including free meals, flexible spaces and a policy that encouraged engineers to spend part of their time on side projects. Its early informal motto, "Don't be evil", appeared in its code of conduct and shaped public discussion about the company's responsibilities. The company has also faced criticism and legal challenges, including antitrust investigations, debates over user privacy and concerns about how its algorithms influence what people see online.

## Summary

From a student research project built around a clever link-analysis algorithm, Google has grown into a technology company whose products touch search, advertising, video, mobile software, productivity tools, cloud infrastructure and artificial intelligence research. Its influence on how people find information and how modern AI systems are built remains substantial.
